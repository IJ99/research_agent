**Cashless Policy and Small Businesses in Nigeria – 2026 Review**  
*The Central Bank of Nigeria’s cashless initiative seeks to curb cash‑dependency, boost digital financial services, and improve financial inclusion. This report summarises the policy’s framework, the benefits and challenges identified for small enterprises, and the limited empirical evidence available up to 2026.*

---  

## 1. Policy Overview and Objectives  

- The cashless policy was first launched in 2011 and has been revised several times, most recently tightening cash‑withdrawal limits in 2022 [3].  
- It is **not intended to eliminate cash** but to **reduce its circulation** and promote electronic payments for goods, services and transfers [3].  
- The CBN imposes a **cash‑handling charge** on daily withdrawals exceeding ₦500,000 for individuals and ₦3 million for corporate accounts, aiming to incentivise electronic collection [2].  
- Stated objectives include **reducing financial crime, lowering tax avoidance, decreasing payment‑system risk and fostering financial inclusion** [1].

## 2. Anticipated Benefits for Small Enterprises  

| Expected Benefit | Source Evidence |
|------------------|-----------------|
| **Greater market reach** – Internet‑based payment platforms can enable SMEs to sell beyond local markets, supporting entrepreneurship and export‑oriented activities [6]. | [6] |
| **Enhanced transparency and efficiency** – Mobile banking, POS machines and online payment platforms are promoted as means to improve transaction transparency and operational efficiency [5]. | [5] |
| **Potential macro‑economic gains** – Higher digital transaction intensity is positively correlated with overall economic growth, which could create a more favourable environment for SMEs [6]. | [6] |

## 3. Reported Challenges and Cost Implications  

- **Infrastructure deficits** – Many small businesses, especially in rural areas, lack reliable internet, electricity or POS devices, limiting their ability to adopt digital payments [1].  
- **Exclusionary effects** – The cash‑handling charge and withdrawal limits may increase operating costs for businesses that rely heavily on cash [1][2].  
- **Compliance considerations** – Banks are required to facilitate electronic collection for corporate customers, which can involve additional administrative steps for firms [2].  

## 4. Empirical Evidence and Studies  

- **Policy description and objectives** – A 2024 study outlines the introduction of cash‑withdrawal limits (2012 & Dec 2022) as a measure to curb illegal transactions and encourage electronic buying and selling, but it does not present firm‑level profit or revenue data [4].  
- **SME‑focused analysis** – Research on small and medium‑scale enterprises notes that the rollout of mobile banking, POS machines and online platforms is intended to enhance transparency and efficiency, yet the paper primarily discusses policy intentions rather than providing primary survey results [5].  
- **Macro‑level impact** – An analysis of cashless‑policy intensity finds a robust positive relationship between digital payment instruments and Nigeria’s economic growth, suggesting indirect benefits for SMEs, though the study does not isolate SME‑specific outcomes [6].  

Overall, the literature points to **potential advantages** for small businesses but **lacks robust, firm‑level impact assessments**; most evidence remains descriptive or based on macro‑economic proxies.

## 5. Limitations  

- **Source composition** – The majority of material consists of policy documents and academic papers that are largely conceptual; few provide primary surveys of small‑business owners.  
- **Vendor‑type claims** – Several statements (e.g., “mobile banking enhances efficiency”) originate from policy proponents rather than independent impact evaluations.  
- **Temporal coverage** – The most recent empirical studies cited are from 2024; the cashless policy has continued to evolve through 2025‑2026, so newer effects may not be captured.  
- **Geographic focus** – Evidence is skewed toward urban or semi‑urban contexts where digital infrastructure is more prevalent, limiting generalisability to informal sectors in remote areas.
