**Cashless Policy and Small Businesses in Nigeria – Evidence‑Based Assessment**  

*The Central Bank of Nigeria’s cashless policy seeks to curb cash‑dependence, expand digital financial services, and lower payment‑system risks. This report synthesizes the available academic, regulatory and vendor‑derived literature on how the policy is expected to affect, and has affected, small enterprises.*  

---  

### 1. Policy Overview  

- The CBN relaunched the cashless initiative to **reduce financial crime and tax avoidance, decrease cash dependency, advance digital financial services (DFS), lower payment‑system risks, and foster financial inclusion** [1].  
- First introduced in 2011, the policy’s explicit aim is **not to eliminate cash but to shrink its circulation** [3].  
- Recent regulatory refinements impose a **“cash handling charge” on daily withdrawals above ₦500,000 for individuals and ₦3 million for corporate bodies**, with exemptions for government accounts, micro‑finance banks and embassies [2].  
- The policy also **limits daily free cash withdrawals**, encouraging users to adopt electronic payment channels [2][3].  

### 2. Anticipated Benefits for Small Enterprises  

| Expected Benefit | Source‑Based Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Lower transaction costs and greater transparency** | Mobile banking, POS terminals and online payment platforms are promoted as ways to **reduce reliance on physical cash and enhance efficiency** for SMEs [5]. |
| **Risk reduction** | By encouraging electronic payments, the policy **decreases overall payment‑system risks**, which can benefit small firms [1][3]. |
| **Expanded market reach via internet transactions** | Increased “internet transaction intensity” enables SMEs to **tap into national and global customer bases**, a channel highlighted as a growth driver [6]. |

### 3. Implementation Challenges  

- **Infrastructure deficits** – Limited broadband, unreliable power and insufficient POS coverage are identified as **infrastructural challenges** that can hinder digital adoption for small firms [1].  
- **Exclusionary and cost implications** – Small enterprises may face **higher fees for electronic transactions** and **cash‑handling charges** if they lack the necessary devices or bank accounts [1][4].  
- **Compliance burden** – The **cash withdrawal limits introduced in 2012 and again in December 2022** require businesses to manage cash‑flow differently, potentially increasing reliance on bank‑mediated channels [4].  
- **Limited awareness and trust** – Adoption is uneven, with some merchants still preferring cash due to **trust issues and limited digital literacy**, although the sources do not quantify this effect.  

### 4. Empirical Evidence on Business Performance  

- A study of **small‑business performance** under the cashless regime notes that the **withdrawal limits aim to curb illegal transactions and reduce cash usage**, indicating a shift toward electronic payments [4].  
- Macro‑level research finds a **robust positive impact of cashless‑policy instruments (ATM, POS, internet banking) on Nigeria’s economic growth**, which can create a more efficient financial environment for SMEs [6].  
- The cited literature is **predominantly qualitative or macro‑economic**; direct firm‑level measurements of revenue, profitability or employment effects for small businesses are not presented.  

### 5. Limitations  

- The sources are **academic briefs, regulatory documents and vendor‑derived reports** that discuss policy intent, macro‑level outcomes and qualitative observations rather than detailed firm‑level metrics.  
- Several references (e.g., [5]) rely on **broad statements about “enhanced transparency and efficiency” without presenting primary data**.  
- Empirical evidence is limited to **aggregate indicators (e.g., economic growth) and qualitative assessments**, making it difficult to isolate the cashless policy’s specific impact on individual small‑business performance.
