**Cashless Policy and Small Businesses in Nigeria – Impact Assessment**  

*The Central Bank of Nigeria’s (CBN) cashless initiative, introduced through withdrawal‑charge rules, aims to shift transactions from cash to electronic channels. Evidence from academic studies, industry reports, and surveys shows a mixed picture: some small firms experience cost savings and revenue gains, while many confront cash shortages, staffing cuts, and limited digital adoption.*  

---  

### 1. The CBN Cashless Policy – What It Entails  
- The CBN imposes a **cash‑handling charge** on daily withdrawals that exceed **₦500,000 for individuals** and **₦3,000,000 for corporate bodies**【3】.  
- The stated objective is to **reduce the volume of physical cash** in circulation and **encourage electronic payments** for goods, services, and transfers【3】.  

### 2. Reported Benefits for Small Businesses  
| Benefit | Evidence |
|---------|----------|
| **Lower cash‑related fraud and crime** | The Bayelsa‑state study finds a **positive correlation** between the cashless policy and reduced cash‑related fraud, improving overall business security【1】. |
| **Reduced cash‑handling costs** | The same study notes that eliminating large cash volumes cuts the **high costs associated with handling cash**, boosting profitability【1】. |
| **Improved customer satisfaction and sales** | By offering electronic payment options, small firms can **enhance customer satisfaction**, which the Bayelsa research links to **higher sales revenue and market growth**【1】. |
| **Access to banking sector for stabilization measures** | Researchers argue that moving transactions into the banking system gives the CBN **leverage to implement macro‑economic stabilization**, indirectly benefiting businesses that rely on a stable monetary environment【2】. |

### 3. Reported Challenges and Negative Impacts  
| Challenge | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| **Cash shortages leading to staff cuts** | A Business Insider Africa survey reports **37 % of small businesses** had to **lay off staff or reduce working hours** because of cash scarcity after the policy’s rollout【7】. |
| **Forced shift to digital payments amid naira scarcity** | **87 % of firms** said they had to **accept digital or alternative payments** due to limited cash availability【7】. |
| **Delayed or forgone capital investment** | **60 % of respondents** indicated they **lost or postponed capital investments** because of cash‑flow constraints【7】. |
| **Limited digital adoption among SMEs** | A Better Than Cash case study of **600 small‑business suppliers** found **very limited adoption** of digital payments, highlighting a gap between policy intent and SME capability【4】. |
| **Risk of business failure without supportive measures** | Analysts caution that **without adequate stakeholder support**, the cashless policy could **negatively affect small‑scale businesses and even cause failures**【2】. |
| **Bank‑side technology and talent constraints** | Industry commentary notes that **banks struggle with e‑system upgrades and talent retention**, which can impede reliable digital services for small firms【6】. |
| **Questionable overall effectiveness** | A broader academic review raises **doubts about the policy’s effectiveness** in achieving Nigeria’s development goals, suggesting mixed outcomes【5】. |

### 4. Synthesis and Policy Implications  
- **Dual outcomes:** While the policy can **lower fraud risk and cash‑handling expenses**, the **lack of widespread digital infrastructure** and **persistent cash shortages** have forced many SMEs to **cut labor and delay growth projects**.  
- **Geographic variation:** Positive effects are documented in **Bayelsa State**, but evidence from **nationwide surveys** points to **significant hardships** for a majority of small firms【1】【7】.  
- **Need for complementary measures:** To translate the policy’s intended benefits into reality, **capacity‑building for digital payments**, **targeted subsidies for transaction fees**, and **strengthening banks’ technology platforms** are repeatedly recommended across sources【2】【4】【6】.  

### 5. Limitations  
- **Geographic concentration:** The most detailed performance data come from a **single state (Bayelsa)**, limiting generalizability【1】.  
- **Reliance on surveys and case studies:** Several findings are based on **self‑reported surveys** (e.g., Business Insider Africa) or **case studies of a limited number of firms**, which may not capture the full spectrum of SME experiences【4】【7】.  
- **Vendor‑type narratives:** Some sources (e.g., Better Than Cash) present **early‑adopter success stories** that may reflect **larger firms rather than typical SMEs**【4】.  
- **Sparse quantitative analysis:** The academic papers provide **descriptive statistics** but lack rigorous causal inference, making it difficult to isolate the policy’s impact from other macro‑economic factors【5】.
