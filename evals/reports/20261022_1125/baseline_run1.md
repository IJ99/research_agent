**AI‑Driven Fraud Detection in African Fintech**  
AI is being positioned as a core tool for combating financial fraud across Africa’s rapidly expanding fintech sector. Experts and industry players cite real‑time analytics, adaptive learning, and automation as key advantages, while vendors describe layered architectures that integrate identity checks, risk scoring, and continuous monitoring.

---

## 1. Why AI Matters for African Fintech  
- Experts argue that AI’s real‑time analytics, adaptive learning, and automation make it “one of the most effective tools against financial fraud” for fintechs operating in Africa [1].  
- The OECD’s 2025 Africa Capital Markets Report lists fraud detection and prevention as a prominent AI use case that can support broader financial inclusion [3].

## 2. Typical Technical Architecture  
A common five‑layer architecture described by Dojah includes [2]:  

| Layer | Function |
|-------|----------|
| **Identity verification** | Validate user credentials (e.g., ID documents, biometrics) |
| **Signal aggregation** | Gather behavioural, device, and transaction signals |
| **AI‑powered risk scoring** | Produce a fraud probability score for each request |
| **Real‑time decisioning** | Approve, flag, or reject activities instantly |
| **Continuous monitoring** | Track post‑decision activity for signs of abuse |

Additional insights:  

- Dojah emphasizes the need for **localized AI models** that reflect African transaction patterns, noting that fraud can look different across markets [9].  
- Eightgen’s case study describes a **multi‑layered, real‑time system** that analyzes hundreds of features, combines supervised and unsupervised learning, operates in under 200 ms per transaction, and includes an adaptive feedback loop that continuously improves detection [10].

## 3. Vendor Solutions in Africa  
| Vendor | Offering (as described by the source) | African Context |
|--------|---------------------------------------|-----------------|
| **Dojah** | Provides an integrated platform covering the five layers above, enabling fintechs to replace fragmented tooling with a unified risk engine [2]. | Targets fintechs across Sub‑Saharan Africa. |
| **Sumsub** | Markets an AI‑driven fraud prevention tool that promises “unmatched accuracy” and automation to support scaling onboarding processes [7]. | Highlights customers such as Danapay and Wirex that operate in African markets (vendor claim). |
| **Mangopay** | Offers real‑time notifications on transaction recommendations and granular fraud‑inquiry data to help platforms mitigate fraud while minimizing friction for legitimate users [8]. | Serves global platforms; the source does not specify African deployments but the technology is applicable. |

## 4. Reported Benefits and Outcomes  
- **Real‑time detection**: AI enables instant flagging of suspicious activity, shortening the window for loss [1][10].  
- **Adaptive learning**: Systems that incorporate feedback loops can continuously refine detection models as new patterns emerge [10].  
- **Support for inclusion**: By strengthening fraud defenses, AI can help expand access to digital credit and payment services for underserved populations [3].

## 5. Limitations and Evidence Gaps  
- **Vendor‑centric information**: Detailed architectural descriptions and performance claims largely come from vendor marketing materials (e.g., Dojah, Sumsub, Mangopay) rather than independent evaluations [2][7][8].  
- **Sparse independent data**: The OECD report provides a high‑level overview of AI use cases but lacks quantitative evidence on fraud loss reductions or model performance in specific African countries [3].  
- **Geographic coverage**: Sources focus on a limited set of markets (primarily Sub‑Saharan fintechs and a few global vendors) and do not capture the full diversity of Africa’s fintech ecosystem.  
- **Temporal relevance**: Some announcements (e.g., BNM/PayNet in Malaysia) are unrelated to Africa and therefore do not inform the regional picture [5].
